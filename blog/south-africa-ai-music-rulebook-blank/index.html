South Africa's AI music rulebook is blank: what independent artists should know

By Justin Erasmus · 2026-10-31

#ai-music#music-rights#south-africa

TechCentral's deputy editor Fanie van Rooyen put it plainly in a feature on 19 October 2026: AI music is here and the South African rulebook is blank. I was one of the people interviewed for it. Here is what the article found, and what I think independent artists should do while the rules are being written.

What the rules say today

  • SAMRO wants disclosure, not a threshold. Members must now disclose how much AI went into a song when registering it, but there is no minimum level of human contribution, and disclosure “does not, in itself, constitute a determination of copyright eligibility or an entitlement to royalties”. In an April survey, 89.2% of 502 SAMRO members said it should push for an AI protection law.
  • Membership is not approval of your process. SAMRO told TechCentral that membership “does not, in itself, constitute confirmation of the copyright status of any particular work or approval of a specific music creation process”. I am a SAMRO and SAMPRA member, and I have registered one song so far. Those are separate things.
  • The SABC is still deciding. Its stations avoid airing fully AI-generated works; on AI-assisted music, its position “is not a finalised organisational policy”.
  • The tool makers are in court. Warner Music Group settled with Suno in November 2025 and signed a licensing deal; Universal and Sony sued again in September 2026.
  • Platforms are flooded. Deezer said fully AI-generated tracks exceeded 50% of daily uploads at their June peak, yet drew only 1–3% of streams.

What I'd tell an independent artist

  1. Document your human contribution. Keep lyric drafts, version histories and notes on what you changed. If a rights body or radio station asks, you have evidence.
  2. Disclose honestly. Say what the AI did: vocals, instrumentation, lyrics or production.
  3. Read your tool's licence. Commercial permission from a paid plan doesn't automatically guarantee copyright protection.
  4. Don't confuse volume with audience. With tens of thousands of AI tracks uploaded every day, quality and identity are what get noticed.
  5. Don't overclaim. Say what you can prove. I say Echo Of The Streets, released on 11 February 2025, was South Africa's first AI album; TechCentral reports the release dates that support the timeline, and I'm careful to present it as my claim.

The rules will keep changing. Until they settle, the safest position for any artist is a clear record of what you made, what the AI made, and how you disclosed it.
