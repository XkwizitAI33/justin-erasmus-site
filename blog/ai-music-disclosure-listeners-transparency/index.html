If listeners can't tell, labels matter: AI-music disclosure in South Africa

By Justin Erasmus · 2026-11-10

#ai-music#music-rights#transparency

If you can't tell by listening, you need another way to know what you are hearing. That is the case for disclosure in AI music, and it is one I have a personal stake in, because I make hybrid music with AI tools and I release it under my own name.

The ear test is already lost

In a 2025 Deezer and Ipsos blind test of 9,000 people in eight countries, 97% could not correctly pick out which of three tracks were fully AI-generated. I'm not citing that to argue that AI music is good. I cite it because it means that "I would know it if I heard it" is no longer a safe position for listeners, platforms or rights bodies.

If the ear can't settle the question, the record has to. That means being clear about what the AI did and what the human did.

What the rules say today

As TechCentral reported on 19 October 2026 in “AI music is here and the South African rulebook is blank”, the picture in South Africa is unfinished:

  • SAMRO asks for disclosure, not a threshold. Members must disclose how much AI went into a song when registering it, but there is no minimum level of human contribution. Disclosure “does not, in itself, constitute a determination of copyright eligibility or an entitlement to royalties”.
  • The SABC has no finalised policy. Its stations avoid airing fully AI-generated works; on AI-assisted music, its position “is not a finalised organisational policy”.
  • Membership is not an endorsement. I am a member of SAMRO and SAMPRA. That is a statement of fact about my membership, nothing more, and it does not mean anyone has approved how I make music.

I covered this in more detail in what independent artists should know. Here I want to focus on what disclosure could look like in practice.

Four questions every release should answer

These are my own suggestions, not a standard that anybody has adopted. For each release, I think a listener or a rights body should be able to find out:

  1. Who wrote the words? I write my own lyrics. AI helps me analyse structure, rhyme and syllables, and then I rewrite them.
  2. What did AI generate? Instrumentation, vocals, or other production elements, named plainly.
  3. Which tool was used? Suno is the central generation tool in my workflow. Tools change, and so do their licences, so naming the tool is part of the record.
  4. What did a human decide? Which version was chosen, what was changed and who finished the master.

This is exactly what the 4-Stage Framework of Concept, Generation, Human Curation and Production & Delivery is meant to make visible. It is a way of showing the human contribution, not hiding it.

Two audiences, two kinds of disclosure

Rights bodies and listeners need different things. SAMRO's disclosure happens when a member registers a song, and it exists so the organisation can see how much AI was involved. Listeners are a different audience: they meet music on a streaming app, where all they see is a title, a cover and a name. Deezer has said that it detects and labels fully AI-generated content on its platform, which shows that labelling is something platforms are already experimenting with.

For a hybrid artist, the useful thing is to be consistent across both. If my description of a record to SAMRO and my description of it to a listener don't match, one of them is wrong.

Why transparency helps artists too

It is tempting to treat disclosure as a burden that only exists to catch artists out. I see it the other way. When so many tracks are uploaded every day, a documented process is one of the few things that separates careful work from the flood. Being open about the AI also takes the fight out of the question and lets the work be judged on its merits.

Disclosure also means not overclaiming

Transparency applies to what artists say about themselves as well. I say that Echo Of The Streets, released on 11 February 2025 by Prismatic Wave, was South Africa's first AI album. That is my claim, and I present it as such. What can be checked is the date: TechCentral reports that Spotify and Apple Music date the album to 11 February 2025, more than seven months before a later 2025 album widely reported as the country's first AI album. Who gets to be called “first” is a question about how history gets recorded, and platform dates are better evidence than headlines.

The same goes for counts. By my own count, I have released 11 albums and 5 EPs in 13 months, plus 20 singles. Apple Music lists 37 releases. When the numbers come from the artist, say so.

What I'd like to see

  • Simple, standard labels that say what AI contributed, similar to the way credits already list who played what.
  • Clear guidance from rights bodies on what to declare and how, so that honest creators don't have to guess.
  • Conversation that includes creators. The people most affected by the rules are the ones who are living them now.

I don't have all the answers, and I'm wary of anyone who says they do. But I would rather document my process in public and be corrected than wait for a rulebook that has not been written yet.
