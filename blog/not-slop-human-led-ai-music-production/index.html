Not slop: what human-led AI music production actually involves

By Justin Erasmus · 2026-11-10

#ai-music#hybrid-production#prismatic-wave

“AI slop” is a fair name for content where nobody is steering. Someone types a prompt, takes whatever comes out and uploads it. If that were all that AI music was, I would be among its critics.

That is not how I work. I make hybrid music: a person sets the direction, AI helps generate options, and a person curates, produces and delivers the result. I can walk anyone through where the human decisions happen, so here it is in writing, as a set of common myths and what I would say to each.

Myth: “It's one prompt and one click”

My process starts and ends with a human. The AlgoRythm 4-Stage Hybrid Music Framework has four stages: Concept, Generation, Human Curation and Production & Delivery. A human sets the concept, AI assists with generation, and a human curates, produces and delivers the final master.

In practice, generation is iterative. As TechCentral summarised my method, a single song typically needs eight to 15 distinct instrumental versions, and complex tracks 20 or more. Most of what gets generated never gets released. That is not a one-click workflow.

Myth: “The AI is the artist”

I write my own lyrics. As TechCentral describes it, I write the raw lyrics myself, use AI to analyse structure, rhyme and syllables, and then rewrite everything. AI is a tool, not the artist. Prismatic Wave is a hybrid artist identity that I created and direct.

On SAfm's The National Briefing with Cathy Mohlahlana on 30 October 2026, the way I put it was: “I'm not looking at it as AI music. I'm looking at it as hybrid music… a combination of human creativity and human intuition.”

Myth: “There's no human work worth protecting”

I am a member of SAMRO and SAMPRA. Membership has limits worth being plain about, and SAMRO told TechCentral that membership “does not, in itself, constitute confirmation of the copyright status of any particular work or approval of a specific music creation process”. The same reporting says SAMRO now requires members to disclose AI use when they register songs, and sets no minimum human contribution.

So nobody has signed off on my process, and I don't claim they have. What I can do is document it. My method is written up in a 2025 white paper, The AlgoRythm Framework, so that anyone can see where the human contribution sits.

Myth: “Volume means no care”

This one is the hardest, and I won't pretend otherwise. By my own count, I have released 11 albums and 5 EPs in 13 months, plus 20 singles. Apple Music lists 37 Prismatic Wave releases. That is a lot of music, and it is fair to ask how quality and identity survive that pace.

My honest answer is that the four stages are the control. Every release goes through the same human decisions: what to make, what to keep, what to throw away and what to finish. I wrote about the numbers in what I learned releasing 37 records in 13 months, including the uncomfortable point that output is not audience.

The four stages in plain terms

It helps to say what each stage is for, because “AI music” hides all of this. Concept is human vision plus AI exploration: I decide what the record is about before any tool is opened. Generation is AI-assisted creation, and it is the only stage where AI is the main actor. Human Curation is producer intervention: the listening, rejecting and combining. Production & Delivery is the final hybrid master. The shape is human, then AI, then human, then audience.

Only one of those four stages is AI-led, and even there I am the one asking the questions and deciding what is worth keeping.

Where critics have a point

Some of the criticism is deserved, and I would rather say so. Platforms are flooded with fully AI-generated tracks. Deezer said they exceeded 50% of daily uploads at their June peak, yet drew only 1–3% of streams. Plenty of that material has nobody steering it.

Two things follow. First, the line between slop and hybrid music cannot be drawn by the tool; it comes from how it is used. Second, “human-led” is a claim, and a claim should be checkable. I cannot prove taste in a blog post. What I can offer is a documented process, honest counts and the music itself on Spotify, Apple Music and Deezer, for you to judge.

Questions I'd want asked

If you are interviewing me, or anyone who works this way, these are the questions that separate the two camps:

  1. Where exactly does the human come in, and what does the AI never do in your studio?
  2. What do you reject, and how much of what you generate never ships?
  3. Who wrote the lyrics, and can you show the drafts?
  4. What do you disclose to listeners and to rights bodies?

I go through these and more in the Press & Story Pack. And I believe the answer to the slop worry is not to keep AI out of the studio. It is to keep people in charge of it, one release at a time.
